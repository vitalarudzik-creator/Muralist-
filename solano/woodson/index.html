Carter G. Woodson

December 19, 1875 – April 3, 1950

The son of formerly enslaved parents, he taught himself, worked in coal mines, started high school at 20 and went on to earn a PhD from Harvard. Then he made sure America could never forget Black history again.

A late start

Woodson was born in New Canton, Virginia, to James and Eliza Woodson, who had both been enslaved. The family was poor, and as a boy he could attend school only a few months a year. He taught himself most of the basics.

As a teenager he worked in the coal mines of West Virginia. There he met older miners, many of them Civil War veterans, who told him stories that never appeared in any textbook. He later said those conversations shaped his life's work.

Catching up, then going further

At 20 he finally enrolled in high school and finished in less than two years. He studied at Berea College, taught in the Philippines, earned his bachelor's and master's degrees at the University of Chicago, and in 1912 received a PhD in history from Harvard. He was only the second African American to do so, after W.E.B. Du Bois.

Writing Black history into the record

Woodson saw that schools and history books either ignored Black Americans or misrepresented them. In 1915 he co-founded the Association for the Study of Negro Life and History, and a year later launched The Journal of Negro History to publish serious research.

In February 1926 he launched Negro History Week. He chose the second week of February because it held the birthdays of Abraham Lincoln and Frederick Douglass, which Black communities already celebrated. Schools, churches and clubs across the country joined in.

Why he is on this wall

Woodson proved that a late start does not decide where you finish. Many students at a community college are working, raising families or returning to school later in life. His story is theirs too: keep learning, and make sure your history is told.
